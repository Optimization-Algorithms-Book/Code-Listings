What is geometric deep learning?

Geometric deep learning (GDL) extends deep learning to data with an underlying geometric structure that is not a regular grid. Click a layer to read its definition.

GDL combines graph theory and deep learning. A graph \(G = (V, E)\) is a structured data type with three sets: vertices or nodes (entities that hold information), edges (connections between nodes, which can hold information too) and the relations that say which vertices each edge joins. The adjacency matrix \(A\) stores these relations, with \(A_{ij} = 1\) when nodes \(i\) and \(j\) are linked, and the degree of a node is its number of neighbors. Euclidean vs non-Euclidean data

Convolutional neural networks work on Euclidean data with a regular structure: an image is a 2D grid of pixels and a text is a 1D sequence of words. Both are special cases of graphs in which every node has the same number of neighbors in the same order, so one fixed-size filter can slide over them. In a general graph the number of neighbors varies and they have no natural order, so the filter must be replaced by an operation that works for any neighborhood, such as summing or averaging the neighbors’ messages.

Section 11.3 of the book explains why graph data is non-Euclidean and compares graph-embedding methods.

Further reading: J. Zhou et al., “Graph neural networks: a review of methods and applications,” arXiv:1812.08434.

The feed-forward approach on a real trip

Take a drive from KFUPM in Dhahran to KFUPM Beach in Al Khobar, where a map app offers three routes. A feed-forward approach breaks the chosen route into road segments, passes the feature vector of each segment through the network once at a time to get its travel time \(t_i\), and sums the results: \(\text{ETA} = \sum_{i=1}^{6} t_i\). Pick a route on the map or in the list, then press Predict ETA.

Our stand-in for the trained network computes \(t_i = 60\,x_1/x_3 + 0.1\,x_4\) minutes, calibrated to the 28, 30 and 31 minutes a map app showed for the three routes in usual traffic; a real model learns how to weigh all the features from data. Notice the hidden assumption: every segment is predicted as an independent sample, yet a jam on one segment soon spills onto its neighbors. Treating the road network as a graph, next, lifts that assumption.

From OpenStreetMap to a road graph

Road graphs are often built from OpenStreetMap, whose data model has three elements: nodes (points such as intersections, trees, bus stops and street signs), ways (lines such as streets, rivers and railway tracks) and relations (groups of elements, for example polygons with holes, administrative boundaries and bus routes). The road graph is then described by node features, the adjacency matrix \(A\) and the degree matrix \(D\), from which graph embeddings are learned. Training and inference

    
The graph embedding is a learned mapping: segments that are similar in the road graph land close together in the embedding space. During training, the road graph goes through the graph embedding and a feed-forward network that predicts the time \(\hat{y}_i\) of each segment on a route. When a car actually drives the route, its travel time is the ground truth, and backpropagation minimizes
      \[ \mathcal{L} = \left| t_{\text{actual}} - \sum_{i=1}^{n} \hat{y}_i \right|, \]
      where \(n\) is the number of road segments in the route. At inference, road embeddings rarely change, so they are stored in a lookup table, the road embedding mapper, and each query only combines them with live and historical data.

Graph embedding

    

      
Neural networks work with vectors, not graphs. Graph embedding maps each node, or a whole graph, to a dense low-dimensional vector that keeps what matters about its place in the graph. This part shows where embeddings sit in a learning pipeline for optimization problems, how an encoder is trained to preserve similarity, and how DeepWalk and GraphSAGE compute embeddings.

      
Section 11.3 of the book explains why graph data is non-Euclidean and compares graph-embedding methods.

    


    
From grids and sentences to graphs

    
A generic five-step pipeline uses graph neural networks to solve combinatorial problems, and it has been applied to the TSP, minimum vertex cover, MaxCut, maximum independent set, vehicle routing, SAT and more. Its second step, Embed, is the subject of this part; the TSP part runs the whole pipeline. Click a step.

The embed step is a convolution on graphs. A CNN slides a discrete kernel, one weight per relative position, over a grid. A GNN cannot do that, because a node’s neighborhood is irregular, so it passes messages through a continuous kernel: one shared function applied to every neighbor. A Transformer is message passing on a fully connected graph, where attention computes how much word \(i\) influences word \(j\). Why embeddings?

    
Words show the idea. Give each word a dense vector whose dimensions mean something, such as living being, feline, human, gender, royalty, verb and plural. Reduced to two dimensions, similar words land close together, and relations become directions: man to woman runs parallel to king to queen. Click two words to compare them.

Encoders and similarity

    

      
An encoder maps each node \(u\) to a low-dimensional vector \(\mathbf{z}_u = \mathrm{ENC}(u)\) so that similarity in the graph survives in the embedding space, \(S_G(u,v) \approx S_E(\mathbf{z}_u,\mathbf{z}_v)\). It takes four steps: define an encoder \(\mathrm{ENC}(v_i)\), a similarity in the graph \(S_G(v_i,v_j)\) and a similarity in the embedding space \(S_E(\mathbf{z}_i,\mathbf{z}_j)\), then optimize the parameters of ENC so that \(S_G \approx S_E\).

      
Hamilton, Ying and Leskovec frame this as a generalized encoder-decoder. A graph \(G = (V, E)\) has a binary adjacency matrix \(A\) and a node attribute matrix \(X\), and the goal is to map each node, or a subgraph, to a vector \(\mathbf{z}\): node embedding or graph embedding. The encoder maps node \(v_i\) to \(\mathbf{z}_i\) from its position, neighborhood and/or attributes; the decoder recovers information such as its neighbors or its community label. The embeddings then feed tasks such as node classification and link prediction. Below, a direct encoding (one trainable row of \(\mathbf{Z}\) per node) minimizes

      \[ \mathcal{L} = \sum_{i \lt j} \big(\mathrm{DEC}(\mathbf{z}_i,\mathbf{z}_j) - s_G(v_i,v_j)\big)^2 . \]

Random walks on the karate club (DeepWalk)

    

      
Zachary’s karate club has 34 members, with an edge between two members who interacted outside the club. A conflict between the administrator, “John A,” and the instructor, “Mr. Hi,” split the club in two: half the members formed a new club around Mr. Hi, and the others found a new instructor or gave up karate. The input is the graph, where similarity is adjacency (the rows of the 0/1 adjacency matrix); the output is a 2D point per member, where similarity is Euclidean distance.

      
DeepWalk learns these points as word embeddings are learned in NLP, by context prediction. In “the quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog,” a window of 2 around “fox” gives the pairs (quick, fox), (brown, fox), (jumps, fox) and (over, fox). On a graph, random walks from every vertex play the role of sentences: the walk V1 V2 V5 V3 V1 gives pairs such as (V2, V5) and (V5, V3). A skip-gram model then pulls members that share contexts together.

GraphSAGE: aggregate your neighbors

    

      
GraphSAGE (sample and aggregate) does not store one vector per node: it learns how to build an embedding, in three steps. (1) Collect the neighbors at distance \(k = 1\) and \(k = 2\). (2) Aggregate their feature information, with one aggregator per pass. (3) Decode the graph context and/or a label from the result. Starting from the features \(\mathbf{h}_v^{0} = \mathbf{x}_v\), pass \(k = 1, \dots, K\) computes

      \[ \mathbf{h}_v^{k} = \sigma\big(\mathbf{W}^{k}\,[\mathbf{h}_v^{k-1} \,\Vert\, \mathrm{mean}_{u \in N(v)} \mathbf{h}_u^{k-1}]\big), \]
      
so \(K\), the number of embedding passes, plays the role of the number of layers. Every node gets its own computation graph, because its neighborhood differs. PinSAGE used the idea on the Pinterest graph, with 3 billion nodes and 18 billion edges. Step through it, change \(K\), or click another node.

Transductive or inductive?

    
A lookup table \(\mathbf{Z}\), like the encoder trained above or DeepWalk, is transductive: it learns from a specific set of nodes to a specific set of predictions, so a node that arrives later has no row. An inductive encoder learns a general rule from the training cases, a function of a node’s features and local neighborhood, that applies to unseen nodes and even to whole new graphs.     Part 5 · Section 11.3
    
Graph neural networks

    

      
Graph neural networks (GNNs) adapt existing machine learning techniques to process non-Euclidean, graph-structured data directly as input, so that no information is lost in transforming the data into the Euclidean input that existing techniques require (Leskovec, 2018). A road network becomes an input graph, and a GNN turns it into one embedding per node. Those embeddings then serve link prediction, node classification and graph classification.

    


    
Message passing in a GNN

    

      
Every GNN layer updates a node from its own state and an aggregate of its neighbors’ messages:

      \[ h_v^{k} = f\Big(h_v^{k-1},\ \square_{j \in N(v)} W(X_j)\Big). \]
      
The initial vectors depend on the application (words, pixels or road features); the key is how the neighbors’ representations are aggregated. Click a node, raise \(k\) to watch its receptive field grow, and shuffle its neighbors.

Inside a GCN layer

    
A graph convolutional network (GCN) embeds the node features \(x_v\) into latents \(h_v \in \mathbb{R}^d\) through \(T\) layers, and each layer runs a message, an aggregation and an update function. A head then turns the latents into an answer: \(z_v = f(h_v)\) for node classification, \(z_{vu} = f(h_v, h_u)\) for link prediction, or \(z_G = f\big(\sum_v h_v\big)\) for graph classification. A GCN layer in matrix form

    

      
All nodes update at once with matrix products. Multiplying the features \(H^{0}\) [5×4] by a learnable \(W^{1}\) [4×8] passes each node through a single-layer network; multiplying by \(D^{-1}A\), the adjacency matrix divided by the degrees, averages the neighbors’ results:

      \[ H^{1} = \mathrm{ReLU}\big(D^{-1} A\, H^{0} W^{1} + H^{0} B^{1}\big). \]
      
The embedding size, 8, is arbitrary. The self term has its own learnable \(B^{1}\) [4×8], so its 4 features are projected to the 8 dimensions of the messages. Click a node to see its rows and numbers.

    Part 6 · Section 11.3.2
    
Attention

    

      
Attention lets a model focus on different parts of its input at every step of its output, so the context is preserved from beginning to end. It began in machine translation and now powers graph attention networks and Transformers. Follow it from the encoder-decoder bottleneck to multi-head attention on a graph.

      
Section 11.3.2 of the book derives equations 11.1 and 11.2, explains multi-head attention (Figure 11.10), and links GAT to the Transformer.

    


    
From seq2seq to attention

    
A sequence-to-sequence (seq2seq) model takes a sequence of items (words, letters, time series and so on) and outputs another sequence. In neural machine translation, an encoder RNN reads the input item by item and compiles what it captures into a context vector, whose size is the number of hidden units of the encoder; a decoder RNN then produces the output item by item. RNNs and LSTMs were the state of the art, but this design has three weaknesses (click a chip). Attention removes the bottleneck: the decoder can use any encoder hidden state, not just one fixed-size vector.

Queries, keys and values

    

      
Attention is a vector of importance weights. To predict or infer one element, such as a pixel or a word, the model estimates how strongly it correlates with (“attends to”) the other elements, then sums their values weighted by the attention vector. In “She is eating a green apple,” the word “eating” attends strongly to “apple” and only weakly to “green.”

      
Each token is embedded, then three learned linear maps turn its embedding into a query, a key and a value, so there are as many \(q\), \(k\) and \(v\) vectors as tokens. The weight matrices are learned by backpropagation and are the same for every token. Click a block.

    

    

      
From a sentence to hidden states
Click a block

      

    

    

      
With \(q = W^{(q)}\acute{q}\), \(k_i = W^{(k)}\acute{k}_i\) and \(v_i = W^{(v)}\acute{v}_i\), where the \(W\)s are fixed-size matrices learned from data, attention maps the inputs \(q\) and \(\{k_i, v_i\}_{i=1}^{N}\) to

      \[ y = \mathrm{Attention}(q, k, v) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i v_i, \qquad \alpha_i = f(q, k_i), \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i = 1, \]
      
for example \(\{\alpha_i\}_{i=1}^{N} = \mathrm{softmax}\big(\{\tanh(q^{T} k_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}\big)\). Click a word to make it the query. In “The bank of the river was flooded,” the score between “bank” and “river” is high, so the output for “bank” becomes a context-aware representation: a river shore, not a financial institution.

Additive, global, local and self-attention

    

      
Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio (2014) introduced additive attention for translation. A bidirectional RNN encodes the source \(x_1, \dots, x_T\) into states \(h_1, \dots, h_T\) that read the sentence both ways. An attention layer, parameterized by a simple feed-forward network, scores each \(h_i\) and normalizes the scores into global alignment weights \(a_{t,1}, \dots, a_{t,T}\); their weighted sum is a dynamic context vector, recomputed at every step. The decoder RNN goes from \(s_{t-1}\) to \(s_t\) using its previous state and that context, and emits \(y_t\). Computing the context and attention vectors at every step makes the cost quadratic.

      
    
        
  • Global attention attends to the entire input state space (Xu et al., 2015; Luong et al., 2015).
    • 
        
  • Local attention attends to part of it, such as a patch of an image: the model predicts an aligned position \(p_t\) and uses a window \([p_t - D, p_t + D]\) around it.
    • 
        
  • Self-attention, or intra-attention, lets the inputs interact with each other (“self”) and find out whom to pay more attention to (“attention”). Queries, keys and values all come from the same set, and the outputs aggregate these interactions and attention scores.
    • 
        
  • Soft attention weighs every position, so it is differentiable but expensive; hard attention picks one position (one patch), so it is cheaper but not differentiable.
    •

Graph attention networks

    

      
A graph attention network (GAT) computes the hidden representation of each node by attending over its neighbors, following a self-attention strategy (Veličković et al., 2017). The operation is efficient, since it runs in parallel across node-neighbor pairs; it handles nodes of different degrees by giving arbitrary weights to the neighbors; and it applies to inductive problems, including generalizing to completely unseen graphs.

      
A graph attentional layer takes \(\mathbf{h} = \{\vec h_1, \dots, \vec h_N\}\), \(\vec h_i \in \mathbb{R}^{F}\), for \(N\) nodes with \(F\) features each, and outputs \(\mathbf{h}' = \{\vec h'_1, \dots, \vec h'_N\}\), \(\vec h'_i \in \mathbb{R}^{F'}\). A learnable linear map \(\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{F' \times F}\), applied to every node, gives enough expressive power. Self-attention on the nodes then scores the importance of node \(j\)’s features to node \(i\), and a softmax over the neighbors \(\mathcal{N}_i\) normalizes the scores so they sum to 1 and can be compared across nodes:

      \[ e_{ij} = a(\mathbf{W}\vec h_i, \mathbf{W}\vec h_j) = \mathrm{LeakyReLU}\big(\vec a^{T}[\mathbf{W}\vec h_i \,\Vert\, \mathbf{W}\vec h_j]\big), \qquad \alpha_{ij} = \mathrm{softmax}_j(e_{ij}) = \frac{\exp(e_{ij})}{\sum_{k \in \mathcal{N}_i} \exp(e_{ik})}. \]
      
The attention mechanism \(a\) is a single-layer network with weight vector \(\vec a \in \mathbb{R}^{2F'}\). On the five-node graph of Part 5, this masked attention turns the adjacency matrix into a weighted one, and with features [5×4] and \(W\) [4×8] each layer updates

      \[ h_v^{k} = \sigma\Big(\frac{1}{|N(v)|}\sum_{u \in N(v)} \alpha_u^{k} W^{k} h_u^{k-1} + h_v^{k-1}\Big), \qquad h_5^{1} = \sigma\Big(\frac{W^{1}(\alpha_2^{1} h_2^{0} + \alpha_4^{1} h_4^{0})}{2} + h_5^{0}\Big). \]
      
Here the self term also goes through \(W^{1}\), so its 4 features match the 8 dimensions of the messages, and \(\sigma\) is the logistic sigmoid. Click a node.

Why LeakyReLU in \(e_{ij}\)? \(\mathrm{ReLU}(x) = \max(0, x)\) has two problems: its gradient is 0 for every negative input, so a unit stuck there stops responding to changes in the error or the input (the dying ReLU problem), and its range \([0, \infty)\) lets activations explode. LeakyReLU keeps a small slope for negative inputs, so the gradient never vanishes there. The slope below also feeds the panel above.

Multi-head attention

    

      
A GAT runs \(H\) independent attention heads, each with its own \(W^{i}\) and \(\vec a\). Every head computes its own coefficients (for node 5: \(\alpha_{52}\), \(\alpha_{54}\) and \(\alpha_{55}\), as the node also attends to itself), and the heads’ outputs are concatenated or averaged; averaging is used on the final prediction layer. Then \(\sigma\) is applied:

      \[ \acute{h}_v = \sigma\Big(\frac{1}{H}\sum_{i=1}^{H}\frac{1}{|N(v)|}\sum_{u \in N(v)} \alpha^{i}_{vu} W^{i} h_u + \alpha^{i}_{vv} h_v\Big). \]
      
Several heads stabilize learning (lower variance), capture diverse relationships as each head focuses on a different aspect, add expressive power, make the model more robust to sparse or irregular neighborhoods, and learn in parallel representation subspaces.

    

    

      
Node 5 with H heads
Seeded random weights per head

      

        

        

          
Like asking five friends about a project and listening with several “ears” (how loud, how often they helped, how confident, how close to the topic), then combining what all the ears hear.

          

          
          
Spread (standard deviation) of the averaged \(\alpha_{52}\) over 400 random weight draws: averaging more heads gives a steadier estimate.

# A multilayer perceptron trained by backpropagation and gradient descent (supervised classification)
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
t = rng.uniform(0, np.pi, 200)
X = np.vstack([np.c_[np.cos(t), np.sin(t)], np.c_[1 - np.cos(t), 0.5 - np.sin(t)]]) + 0.1 * rng.standard_normal((400, 2))
y = np.repeat([0, 1], 200)
idx = rng.permutation(400); train, test = idx[:300], idx[300:]

H = 16
W1, b1 = rng.normal(0, 1, (2, H)), np.zeros(H)
W2, b2 = rng.normal(0, 1 / np.sqrt(H), (H, 1)), np.zeros(1)
sigmoid = lambda z: 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))
lr = 0.5


def forward(X):
    h = np.tanh(X @ W1 + b1)
    return h, sigmoid(h @ W2 + b2).ravel()


for epoch in range(2001):
    h, p = forward(X[train])
    loss = -np.mean(y[train] * np.log(p + 1e-12) + (1 - y[train]) * np.log(1 - p + 1e-12))   # cross-entropy
    dz = (p - y[train])[:, None] / len(train)          # gradient of the loss at the output
    dW2, db2 = h.T @ dz, dz.sum(0)
    dh = dz @ W2.T * (1 - h ** 2)                       # backpropagate through tanh
    dW1, db1 = X[train].T @ dh, dh.sum(0)
    W1 -= lr * dW1; b1 -= lr * db1; W2 -= lr * dW2; b2 -= lr * db2
    if epoch % 500 == 0:
        acc = lambda s: np.mean((forward(X[s])[1] > 0.5) == y[s])
        print(f"epoch {epoch:4d}: loss {loss:.3f}, train accuracy {acc(train):.1%}, test accuracy {acc(test):.1%}")

relu = lambda z: np.maximum(0, z)
leaky = lambda z, a=0.2: np.where(z > 0, z, a * z)
print("ReLU(-2), ReLU(3):", relu(-2.0), relu(3.0), "  LeakyReLU(-2), LeakyReLU(3):", leaky(-2.0), leaky(3.0))

# The convex hull as a pointer sequence (Section 11.9, Figures 11.11 and 11.19)
import math
import random

rng = random.Random(4)
P = [(rng.random(), rng.random()) for _ in range(12)]


def cross(o, a, b):
    return (a[0] - o[0]) * (b[1] - o[1]) - (a[1] - o[1]) * (b[0] - o[0])


def jarvis(P):
    """Gift wrapping: start at the leftmost point and keep turning to the most clockwise point."""
    start = min(range(len(P)), key=lambda i: P[i])
    hull, cur = [], start
    while True:
        hull.append(cur)
        nxt = (cur + 1) % len(P)
        for j in range(len(P)):
            if cross(P[cur], P[nxt], P[j]) < 0:
                nxt = j
        cur = nxt
        if cur == start:
            return hull


def graham(P):
    """Graham scan: sort by angle around the lowest point, then keep only left turns."""
    o = min(range(len(P)), key=lambda i: (P[i][1], P[i][0]))
    rest = sorted((i for i in range(len(P)) if i != o), key=lambda i: math.atan2(P[i][1] - P[o][1], P[i][0] - P[o][0]))
    st = [o]
    for i in rest:
        while len(st) > 1 and cross(P[st[-2]], P[st[-1]], P[i]) <= 0:
            st.pop()
        st.append(i)
    return st


def area(idx):
    pts = [P[i] for i in idx]
    return abs(sum(pts[i][0] * pts[i - 1][1] - pts[i - 1][0] * pts[i][1] for i in range(len(pts)))) / 2


h1, h2 = jarvis(P), graham(P)
print("Jarvis march hull:", [i + 1 for i in h1])
print("Graham scan hull: ", [i + 1 for i in h2], f"(the same {len(h2)} points, area {area(h2):.3f})")
# A pointer network outputs this sequence one index at a time: at step i, a softmax over the inputs
# (equations 11.3 to 11.5) "points" to the next hull vertex, and the sequence ends with an <eos> token.
print("pointer targets:", " -> ".join(str(i + 1) for i in h1), "-> <eos>")
print("book's test results (accuracy, hull overlap) by points per sample: 5: 54.8%, 43.7% | 20: 84.8%, 92.7% | 45: 72.4%, 90.4%")

      
# The end-to-end pipeline of Figure 11.16 on a small TSP: edge probabilities, then greedy or beam-search decoding
import math
import random
from itertools import combinations

rng = random.Random(2)
n = 10
P = [(rng.random(), rng.random()) for _ in range(n)]
D = [[math.dist(a, b) for b in P] for a in P]
tour_len = lambda t: sum(D[t[i]][t[(i + 1) % n]] for i in range(n))

# Stand-in for the trained GNN's "predict" step: edge scores from distances (a k-nearest-neighbor softmax).
# A real model learns these probabilities from Concorde's optimal tours.
prob = [[0.0] * n for _ in range(n)]
for i in range(n):
    near = sorted((j for j in range(n) if j != i), key=lambda j: D[i][j])[:4]
    z = [math.exp(-D[i][j] / 0.1) for j in near]
    for j, w in zip(near, z):
        prob[i][j] = w / sum(z)


def beam_search(width):
    beams = [([0], 0.0)]
    for _ in range(n - 1):
        cand = [(t + [j], s + math.log(prob[t[-1]][j] + 1e-9)) for t, s in beams for j in range(n) if j not in t]
        beams = sorted(cand, key=lambda c: -c[1])[:width]
    return min((t for t, _ in beams), key=tour_len)            # pick the shortest of the surviving tours


def held_karp():
    """Exact dynamic programming, O(2^n n^2): the ground truth (Concorde's role in the book)."""
    best = {(1 << j, j): (D[0][j], [0, j]) for j in range(1, n)}
    for size in range(2, n):
        for sub in combinations(range(1, n), size):
            mask = sum(1 << j for j in sub)
            for j in sub:
                prev = mask ^ (1 << j)
                best[(mask, j)] = min(((best[(prev, k)][0] + D[k][j], best[(prev, k)][1] + [j]) for k in sub if k != j), key=lambda x: x[0])
    full = sum(1 << j for j in range(1, n))
    return min((best[(full, j)][0] + D[j][0], best[(full, j)][1]) for j in range(1, n))


opt, _ = held_karp()
for w in (1, 3, 10):
    print(f"beam width {w:2d} ({'greedy' if w == 1 else 'beam search'}): {tour_len(beam_search(w)):.4f}")
print(f"optimal (Held-Karp): {opt:.4f}")

      
# Amortized optimization (learning to optimize), a one-dimensional version of Listing 11.3 on the unit circle
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
K = 4                                               # components per function, as in c_convex(n_components=4)


def make_problem(rng):
    ys = rng.uniform(-np.pi, np.pi, K)              # component centers on the circle
    alphas = 0.7 * rng.random(K)                    # offsets in [0, 0.7)
    return np.concatenate([np.cos(ys), np.sin(ys), alphas])     # the parameter vector


def f(theta, p, gamma=0.5):
    """Soft-min of 0.5 d(theta, y_k)^2 + alpha_k, with d the arc (geodesic) distance."""
    ys = np.arctan2(p[K:2 * K], p[:K]); al = p[2 * K:]
    d = np.abs((theta - ys + np.pi) % (2 * np.pi) - np.pi)
    c = 0.5 * d ** 2 + al
    return -gamma * np.log(np.exp(-c / gamma).sum())


train = [make_problem(rng) for _ in range(64)]
test = [make_problem(rng) for _ in range(16)]
grid = np.linspace(-np.pi, np.pi, 2000)
solve = lambda p: grid[np.argmin([f(t, p) for t in grid])]     # the slow way: search every time

# model: parameters -> point on the circle, a small MLP whose 2 outputs are normalized like the sphere model
H = 64
W1 = rng.normal(0, 0.3, (3 * K, H)); W2 = rng.normal(0, 0.3, (H, 2)); lr = 0.01
for it in range(3000):
    p = train[rng.integers(len(train))]
    h = np.tanh(p @ W1); z = h @ W2; theta = np.arctan2(z[1], z[0])
    eps = 1e-4; g_theta = (f(theta + eps, p) - f(theta - eps, p)) / (2 * eps)   # loss = f(model(p)), as in the book
    r2 = z @ z; dz = g_theta * np.array([-z[1], z[0]]) / r2                      # d theta / d z
    dh = (W2 @ dz) * (1 - h ** 2)
    W2 -= lr * np.outer(h, dz); W1 -= lr * np.outer(p, dh)

pred = lambda p: (lambda z: np.arctan2(z[1], z[0]))(np.tanh(p @ W1) @ W2)
gap = lambda ps, guess: np.mean([f(guess(p), p) - f(solve(p), p) for p in ps])
print(f"mean gap f(prediction) - f(optimum), 0 is perfect:")
print(f"   trained problems {gap(train[:16], pred):.3f}, unseen problems {gap(test, pred):.3f}, random guess {gap(test, lambda p: rng.uniform(-np.pi, np.pi)):.3f}")
