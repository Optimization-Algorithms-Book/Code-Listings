Part 1 · Source of inspiration

From flocks and schools to a search algorithm

Life on this planet is full of cooperation. Bacteria colonies of 109 to 1012 cells adapt to their surroundings, ants build bridges with their bodies, termites build ventilated mounds, honeybees recruit foragers with the waggle dance, and fireflies attract each other with light. These collective behaviors inspired a family of swarm intelligence (SI) algorithms: ant colony optimization, artificial bee colony, the firefly algorithm, and particle swarm optimization.

Swarm intelligence systems share three key elements:

  • A large number of simple processing elements work without supervision.
  • Elements communicate only with their neighborhood.
  • Convergence is expected, but the time to convergence is uncertain, so most research is experimental.

Particle swarm optimization is a population-based stochastic optimization technique developed by Russell Eberhart and James Kennedy in 1995, inspired by bird flocking and fish schooling. They chose the word particle for an individual (a candidate solution) because it suits the notions of velocity and acceleration. The guiding principle is simple: emulate the success of neighboring individuals. If one particle discovers a good path to food, the rest of the swarm can follow it at once.

“…allows wisdom to emerge rather than trying to impose it…”
J. Kennedy and R. Eberhart, inventors of PSO

Bird flocking: three local rules

Reynolds boids

Separation avoids crowding nearby birds, alignment matches their average heading, and coherence moves toward their average position. Set any weight to zero to see the flock lose that behavior. No bird has global information, yet a flock emerges.

PSO is easy to implement and has few parameters to adjust. Nobody is eliminated: a poor particle keeps flying and keeps learning. Its memory (the personal best) and its social link (the neighborhood best) replace the genetic operators.

The book’s theme-park analogy: a group of friends looking for the most thrilling ride. Each keeps walking in the direction they were already going (inertia), remembers the best ride they have tried (cognitive pull), and is drawn toward the ride a friend is raving about (social pull).

Part 2 · The algorithm

Pseudocode, motion equations and steps

Each particle i holds its current position xi, its velocity vi, its personal best pbesti, and the best position found in its neighborhood, nbest. When the neighborhood is the whole swarm, nbest is the global best gbest; when it is a few particles, it is the local best lbest.

Algorithm: PSO for each particle initialize position x (random) and velocity v (zero or small) pbest ← x end do for each particle calculate fitness f(x) if f(x) is better than f(pbest) # eq. 9.3 pbest ← x end choose nbest (gbest for the star topology) # eq. 9.4 for each particle v ← w·v + c1·r1·(pbest − x) + c2·r2·(nbest − x) # eq. 9.1 x ← x + v # eq. 9.2 end while maximum iterations or minimum error is not reached
  1. Initialize the algorithm parameters (N, w, c1, c2).
  2. Initialize the swarm with random positions and zero or small velocities.
  3. Evaluate the fitness of each particle.
  4. Update each particle’s pbest and each neighborhood’s best.
  5. Generate random numbers r1 and r2 in [0, 1], per dimension.
  6. Update the velocity of each particle (eq. 9.1).
  7. Update the position of each particle (eq. 9.2).
  8. Stopping criteria met? If not, start a new iteration at step 3. Typical criteria: maximum iterations, maximum function evaluations, an acceptable solution, or no improvement over several iterations.
vk+1id = w·vkid + c1r1(pbestkid − xkid) + c2r2(gbestkd − xkid)(9.1)
xk+1id = xkid + vk+1id(9.2)

Here k is the iteration, i the particle and d the dimension. The inertia term reflects that a bird cannot change direction suddenly. The cognitive term pulls the particle toward its own experience, and the social term pulls it toward the swarm’s experience. Drag the points below to build intuition.

Part 4 · Variant 2

Binary PSO

Many real problems are discrete: feature selection, task allocation, scheduling, the TSP. Discrete PSO comes in two flavors: binary PSO discretizes the position vector, and permutation PSO redefines the arithmetic operations.

In binary PSO (Kennedy and Eberhart, 1997) each bit is 0 or 1. The velocity stays a real number, but it is read as the propensity of the bit to be 1. The sigmoid squeezes it into a probability, and a random draw decides the new bit.

vk+1id = vkid + φ1(pbestkid − xkid) + φ2(gbestkd − xkid)(9.8)
sig(v) = 1 / (1 + e−v)(9.9)
xk+1id = 1 if sig(vk+1id) > r, otherwise 0(9.10)

For example, sig(v) = 0.3 means a 30% chance the bit becomes 1 and a 70% chance it becomes 0. A velocity of 0 means a coin flip. φ1 and φ2 are random numbers, often drawn from U(0, 2) so their limits sum to 4.

Part 5 · Variant 3

Permutation-based PSO

For problems such as the TSP, a position is an ordering of cities, for example [2, 4, 6, 1, 5, 3, 8, 7]. Velocity and direction have no natural meaning here, so M. Clerc (2004) redefined the three operations in equation 9.1. A velocity is a list of swaps; each swap (a, b) exchanges cities a and b in the tour.

Subtraction: position − position = velocity

The swaps that turn Position 2 into Position 1 (Figure 9.9).

Addition: position + velocity = position

Apply the swaps in order (Figure 9.10).

Multiplication: constant × velocity

c = 0 empties the list, c < 1 truncates it, c > 1 augments it with swaps taken from the top (Figure 9.8).

With these operators, equation 9.1 reads v ← (w ⊗ v) ⊕ (c1·r1 ⊗ (pbest ⊖ x)) ⊕ (c2·r2 ⊗ (nbest ⊖ x)), where ⊕ joins swap lists. The new length is ⌈c·|v|⌉ for 0 < c < 1 and ⌊c·|v|⌋ for c ≥ 1, which reproduces the book’s figure. Vmax caps the number of swaps applied per move.

Part 6 · Section 9.5

Adaptive PSO

The inertia, cognitive and social components are the main levers for balancing exploration and exploitation. A fixed setting is a compromise. Adaptive PSO changes these parameters while the search runs: it explores broadly at the start and homes in on the best solutions late.

The inertia weight is a particle’s tendency to keep its current trajectory. When w > 1, velocities grow over time toward the maximum velocity, if velocity clamping is used, and the swarm diverges. Particles then struggle to turn back toward promising regions. When w < 1, particles gradually slow down until their velocities approach 0, depending on c1 and c2.

Velocity clamping sets a maximum (and minimum) velocity. If a computed velocity exceeds the limit, it is set to the limit. This keeps particles from wandering far off in the problem space or getting stuck in one region.

Exploration: zoom out large w · c1 > c2

Free candidate solutions spread over the feasible search space, looking for new regions. Overdone, c1 > c2 makes individuals wander excessively.

Exploitation: zoom in small w · c2 > c1

The swarm concentrates on the neighbors of elite solutions and refines them. Overdone, c2 > c1 makes particles rush prematurely toward a local optimum.

After Figure 9.11: effect of PSO parameters on search behavior.

Inertia weight schedules (9.5.1)

  • Random selection (RS) picks a different inertia weight in each iteration. The mean is 0.75, and the swarm must still converge despite the randomness.
  • Linear time varying (LTV) decreases w steadily from wmax to wmin, typically 0.9 and 0.4.
  • Nonlinear time varying (NLTV) also starts high (w0 = 0.9), but falls nonlinearly. It reaches the low end of the range early and stays there, which strengthens local search (exploitation).
(9.11)wt = 0.5 + rand(·) / 2

(9.12)wt = (wmax − wmin) × (tmax − t) / tmax + wmin

(9.13)wt+1 = (wt − wmin) × (tmax − t) / (tmax + wmin) + wmin

rand(·) is uniform on [0, 1], t is the current iteration and tmax the number of iterations.

Cognitive and social components (9.5.2)

c1 measures how much a particle learns from its own experience; c2 measures how much it is pulled by the best solutions of its neighbors. If c1 > c2 the algorithm explores, and if c2 > c1 it exploits the local search space. c1 = 0 gives the social-only (selfless) model, and c2 = 0 gives the cognition-only model, where particles act as independent hill climbers. Keep c1 + c2 ≤ 4 to avoid divergence.

Adaptive PSO decreases c1 and increases c2 linearly, so the swarm starts individualistic and ends cooperative (Figure 9.13 uses c1 from 2.0 to 0.5 and c2 from 0.8 to 2.5).

(9.14)c1t = (c1max − c1min) × (tmax − t) / tmax + c1min

(9.15)c2t = c2max − (c2max − c2min) × (tmax − t) / tmax

# Continuous PSO from scratch (Chapter 9, Section 9.2)
# Minimizes the Michalewicz function with a selectable neighborhood topology.
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt


def michalewicz(x, m=10):
    i = np.arange(1, x.shape[-1] + 1)
    return -np.sum(np.sin(x) * np.sin(i * x**2 / np.pi) ** (2 * m), axis=-1)


def neighbors(topology, n):
    """Indices each particle listens to (itself included)."""
    cols = int(np.ceil(np.sqrt(n)))
    nb = []
    for i in range(n):
        if topology == "star":            # gbest PSO: everyone talks to everyone
            s = range(n)
        elif topology == "ring":          # lbest PSO: left and right neighbors
            s = [i, (i - 1) % n, (i + 1) % n]
        elif topology == "von_neumann":   # grid: left, right, up, down
            s = [i, (i - 1) % n, (i + 1) % n, (i - cols) % n, (i + cols) % n]
        elif topology == "wheel":         # particle 0 is the hub
            s = range(n) if i == 0 else [i, 0]
        nb.append(np.unique(list(s)))
    return nb


def pso(f, bounds, n_particles=20, iters=100, w=0.792, c1=1.494, c2=1.494,
        topology="star", vmax_frac=0.2, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    lo, hi = map(np.asarray, bounds)
    d = lo.size
    x = rng.uniform(lo, hi, (n_particles, d))       # random positions
    v = np.zeros_like(x)                            # zero initial velocity
    vmax = vmax_frac * (hi - lo)
    pbest, pbest_f = x.copy(), f(x)                 # pbest = initial position
    nb = neighbors(topology, n_particles)
    history = [pbest_f.min()]
    for k in range(iters):
        # best position found inside each particle's neighborhood
        nbest = np.array([pbest[idx[np.argmin(pbest_f[idx])]] for idx in nb])
        r1, r2 = rng.random((2, n_particles, d))    # new numbers per dimension
        v = w * v + c1 * r1 * (pbest - x) + c2 * r2 * (nbest - x)   # eq. 9.1
        v = np.clip(v, -vmax, vmax)
        x = np.clip(x + v, lo, hi)                                   # eq. 9.2
        fx = f(x)
        improved = fx < pbest_f                                      # eq. 9.3
        pbest[improved], pbest_f[improved] = x[improved], fx[improved]
        history.append(pbest_f.min())
    g = np.argmin(pbest_f)
    return pbest[g], pbest_f[g], history


bounds = ([0, 0], [np.pi, np.pi])
for topology in ["star", "ring", "von_neumann", "wheel"]:
    best_x, best_f, hist = pso(michalewicz, bounds, topology=topology, seed=1)
    print(f"{topology:12s} x* = {np.round(best_x, 3)}  f* = {best_f:.4f}")
    plt.plot(hist, label=topology)

plt.axhline(-1.8013, ls="--", c="gray", label="known minimum")
plt.xlabel("iteration"); plt.ylabel("best cost"); plt.legend()
plt.title("Michalewicz (d = 2): effect of neighborhood topology")
plt.show()
# Binary PSO from scratch (Chapter 9, Section 9.3)
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

sigmoid = lambda v: 1 / (1 + np.exp(-v))

# 1) The worked example: update bit 3 of particle 4 (100010)
v43, x43, pbest43, gbest3, phi1, phi2, r43 = 0.23, 0, 1, 1, 1.5, 1.9, 0.6
v_new = v43 + phi1 * (pbest43 - x43) + phi2 * (gbest3 - x43)          # eq. 9.8
prob = sigmoid(v_new)                                                  # eq. 9.9
x_new = 1 if prob > r43 else 0                                         # eq. 9.10
print(f"v = {v_new:.2f}, sig(v) = {prob:.3f}, new bit = {x_new}")    # 3.63, 0.974, 1

# 2) A full BPSO run: select projects under a budget (0/1 knapsack)
costs  = np.array([12, 7, 15, 5, 9, 6, 8, 11, 4, 7, 13, 14, 3, 4, 10, 5])
values = np.array([18, 11, 21, 9, 12, 10, 11, 16, 6, 9, 17, 20, 5, 5, 13, 7])
budget = 60

def fitness(X):  # maximize value, penalize budget overruns
    over = np.maximum(0, X @ costs - budget)
    return X @ values - 3 * over


def bpso(n_particles=20, iters=100, w=1.0, c1=2.0, c2=2.0, vmax=4.0, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    d = costs.size
    x = rng.integers(0, 2, (n_particles, d))
    v = np.zeros((n_particles, d))
    pbest, pbest_f = x.copy(), fitness(x)
    history = [pbest_f.max()]
    for k in range(iters):
        g = pbest[np.argmax(pbest_f)]                       # star topology
        r1, r2 = rng.random((2, n_particles, d))
        v = w * v + c1 * r1 * (pbest - x) + c2 * r2 * (g - x)
        v = np.clip(v, -vmax, vmax)                         # keeps sig(v) off 0 and 1
        x = (rng.random((n_particles, d)) < sigmoid(v)).astype(int)
        fx = fitness(x)
        better = fx > pbest_f
        pbest[better], pbest_f[better] = x[better], fx[better]
        history.append(pbest_f.max())
    best = pbest[np.argmax(pbest_f)]
    return best, history


best, hist = bpso(seed=3)
print("selected projects:", np.flatnonzero(best) + 1)
print("cost =", best @ costs, "/", budget, " value =", best @ values)
plt.plot(hist); plt.xlabel("iteration"); plt.ylabel("best value")
plt.title("Binary PSO on project selection"); plt.show()
# Permutation-based PSO from scratch (Chapter 9, Sections 9.4 and 9.6)
# Velocity = list of swaps. Subtraction, addition and multiplication are redefined.
import math
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt


def subtract(target, x):
    """target - x: the swaps that turn x into target (eq. 9.1 differences)."""
    x, swaps = list(x), []
    for i, c in enumerate(target):
        if x[i] != c:
            swaps.append((c, x[i]))
            j = x.index(c)
            x[i], x[j] = x[j], x[i]
    return swaps


def add(x, velocity):
    """x + v: apply each swap (exchange the two cities) in order."""
    x = list(x)
    for a, b in velocity:
        i, j = x.index(a), x.index(b)
        x[i], x[j] = x[j], x[i]
    return x


def multiply(c, velocity):
    """c * v: c = 0 empties, c < 1 truncates, c > 1 augments from the top."""
    n = len(velocity)
    if c <= 0 or n == 0:
        return []
    k = math.ceil(c * n) if c < 1 else math.floor(c * n)
    return [velocity[i % n] for i in range(k)]


# The book's figures 9.8 to 9.10
print(subtract([2, 4, 6, 1, 5, 3, 8, 7], [6, 4, 7, 1, 5, 3, 8, 2]))  # [(2, 6), (6, 7)]
print(add([2, 4, 6, 1, 5, 3, 8, 7], [(1, 3), (4, 7)]))              # [2, 7, 6, 3, 5, 1, 8, 4]
print(multiply(0.4, [(2, 6), (3, 7)]), multiply(1.8, [(2, 6), (3, 7)]))

def haversine_km(i, j):
    a = (np.sin((lat[j] - lat[i]) / 2) ** 2
         + np.cos(lat[i]) * np.cos(lat[j]) * np.sin((lon[j] - lon[i]) / 2) ** 2)
    return 2 * 6371.0088 * np.arcsin(np.sqrt(a))

tour_length = lambda t: sum(D[t[k], t[(k + 1) % n]] for k in range(n))


def two_opt(t):
    """Optional local search: reverse segments while the tour gets shorter."""
    t, improved = t[:], True
    while improved:
        improved = False
        for i in range(1, n - 1):
            for j in range(i + 1, n):
                a, b, c, d = t[i - 1], t[i], t[j], t[(j + 1) % n]
                if D[a, c] + D[b, d] < D[a, b] + D[c, d] - 1e-9:
                    t[i:j + 1] = t[i:j + 1][::-1]
                    improved = True
    return t


def permutation_pso(n_particles=40, iters=300, w=0.3, c1=1.5, c2=1.5,
                    vmax=10, hybrid=False, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    x = [list(rng.permutation(n)) for _ in range(n_particles)]
    v = [[] for _ in range(n_particles)]
    pbest = [p[:] for p in x]
    pbest_f = [tour_length(p) for p in x]
    history = [min(pbest_f)]
    for k in range(iters):
        g = pbest[int(np.argmin(pbest_f))]                   # star topology
        for i in range(n_particles):
            r1, r2 = rng.random(2)
            v[i] = (multiply(w, v[i])
                    + multiply(c1 * r1, subtract(pbest[i], x[i]))
                    + multiply(c2 * r2, subtract(g, x[i])))[:vmax]
            x[i] = add(x[i], v[i])
            f = tour_length(x[i])
            if f < pbest_f[i]:
                pbest[i], pbest_f[i] = x[i][:], f
        if hybrid:                                            # PSO + 2-opt on the best
            b = int(np.argmin(pbest_f))
            pbest[b] = two_opt(pbest[b])
            pbest_f[b] = tour_length(pbest[b])
        history.append(min(pbest_f))
    b = int(np.argmin(pbest_f))
    return pbest[b], pbest_f[b], history

# Adaptive PSO from scratch (Chapter 9, Section 9.5)
# Inertia schedules (eqs. 9.11 to 9.13) and time-varying c1, c2 (eqs. 9.14, 9.15).
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

W_MAX, W_MIN = 0.9, 0.4


def inertia(strategy, t, t_max, w_prev, rng):
    if strategy == "RS":      # random selection, mean 0.75 (eq. 9.11)
        return 0.5 + rng.random() / 2
    if strategy == "LTV":     # linear time varying (eq. 9.12)
        return (W_MAX - W_MIN) * (t_max - t) / t_max + W_MIN
    if strategy == "NLTV":    # nonlinear time varying, w_0 = 0.9 (eq. 9.13)
        if t == 0:
            return W_MAX
        return (w_prev - W_MIN) * (t_max - (t - 1)) / (t_max + W_MIN) + W_MIN
    return 0.792              # constant inertia


def coefficients(t, t_max, c1=(0.5, 2.0), c2=(0.8, 2.5)):
    """c1 falls from c1_max to c1_min, c2 rises from c2_min to c2_max."""
    c1_t = (c1[1] - c1[0]) * (t_max - t) / t_max + c1[0]     # eq. 9.14
    c2_t = c2[1] - (c2[1] - c2[0]) * (t_max - t) / t_max     # eq. 9.15
    return c1_t, c2_t


def rastrigin(x):
    return 10 * x.shape[-1] + np.sum(x**2 - 10 * np.cos(2 * np.pi * x), axis=-1)


def adaptive_pso(f, lo, hi, d=10, n=20, t_max=300, strategy="LTV",
                 tvac=False, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    x = rng.uniform(lo, hi, (n, d))
    v = np.zeros_like(x)
    vmax = 0.2 * (hi - lo)
    pbest, pbest_f = x.copy(), f(x)
    w, history = None, [pbest_f.min()]
    for t in range(t_max):
        w = inertia(strategy, t, t_max, w, rng)
        c1, c2 = coefficients(t, t_max) if tvac else (1.49, 1.49)
        g = pbest[np.argmin(pbest_f)]
        r1, r2 = rng.random((2, n, d))
        v = np.clip(w * v + c1 * r1 * (pbest - x) + c2 * r2 * (g - x), -vmax, vmax)
        x = np.clip(x + v, lo, hi)
        fx = f(x)
        better = fx < pbest_f
        pbest[better], pbest_f[better] = x[better], fx[better]
        history.append(pbest_f.min())
    return pbest_f.min(), history

Part 8 · Summary

What to remember

Continuous PSO

Positions and velocities are real vectors. Inertia, cognitive and social terms set the balance between exploration and exploitation. Star topologies converge fast on unimodal problems; ring and Von Neumann topologies resist premature convergence on multimodal ones.

Binary PSO

Velocity becomes the propensity of a bit to be 1. The sigmoid turns it into a probability, and Vmax keeps that probability away from 0 and 1 so the swarm keeps exploring.

Permutation PSO

Velocity is a list of swaps. Subtraction, addition and multiplication are redefined so equation 9.1 still applies. Pure swap PSO is weak on the TSP; hybridizing with local search such as 2-opt helps a lot.

Adaptive PSO

Change w, c1 and c2 during the run. Random, linear and nonlinear inertia schedules, plus a falling c1 and a rising c2, move the swarm from diversification early to intensification late.

The basic principle: let the swarm move toward the best positions in the search space, while each particle remembers its own best position and the best position known to its neighbors. Adaptive PSO goes one step further and changes w, c1 and c2 during the run (random, linear or nonlinear schedules), starting with exploration and ending with exploitation.
